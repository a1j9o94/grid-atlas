# Grid Atlas editorial guide

Grid Atlas is written for an intelligent reader who knows nothing about electricity markets. Every screen must make sense through a direct link. The reader should never need an earlier card to decode the current one.

## The five questions

The atlas answers five questions in order:

1. **Markets:** Who coordinates wholesale power here?
2. **State rules:** Can a household choose its electricity seller?
3. **Local wires:** Which utility owns the poles and wires?
4. **Your address:** How do those layers meet at one zip code?
5. **History:** Which business, engineering, and legal decisions produced this system?

## Vocabulary

- **Interconnection:** A synchronized electrical machine. The continental United States has three: Eastern, Western, and ERCOT.
- **Market operator:** An independent organization that coordinates wholesale trading and balances supply with demand. RTO and ISO are formal labels for this role.
- **Electricity seller:** The company that sells electricity to the customer. It may be separate from the wires utility.
- **Wires utility:** The local utility that owns the poles, wires, and meters used to deliver electricity.
- **Holding company:** A company that owns other companies. In the history layer, holding companies sat above the local operating utilities that ran plants and wires.
- **Operating utility:** A company that ran power plants, wires, or both for customers.
- **Field of operation:** The counties where a holding-company group operated utilities. It describes operations rather than stock ownership.
- **Transmission:** High-voltage lines that move large amounts of electricity between power plants and local utility systems.
- **Distribution:** Lower-voltage poles and wires that deliver electricity to homes and businesses.

Use **grid** only for the physical electrical system. Use **market** for wholesale trading. Use **territory** for the land served by a wires utility. Use **seller** for the company selling electricity.

## History frame contract

The history is a chain of causes and consequences. Each frame answers four questions in this order:

1. What pressure or problem had developed?
2. What did a company, engineer, regulator, or lawmaker do about it?
3. What changed on the map?
4. What possibility or problem did that create next?

The main body carries the causal story. The note explains only a limit that changes how the map should be read. Event cards add detail without carrying facts required to understand the frame.

Production methods belong in the repository. Do not put tracing procedures, georeferencing, crosswalk construction, reader-agreement statistics, validation thresholds, or pipeline details in a history frame. Keep uncertainty visible in the product when it changes the meaning of a mark.

The narrative spine is scale. Electricity repeatedly grew beyond the institution built to manage it. Transmission outgrew the city system. Interstate corporate control outgrew state regulation. Large physical grids required regional reliability rules. Wholesale competition required neutral access to shared transmission. Today's map contains the settlements left by each response.

## Voice

- Use short declarative sentences.
- Define a technical term on first use.
- Prefer named people, companies, laws, dates, and places.
- Use headers that label the subject.
- Cut setup language and emotional instructions.
- Avoid em dashes, mirrored constructions, and “not X, but Y” sentences.
- End when the explanation is complete.
